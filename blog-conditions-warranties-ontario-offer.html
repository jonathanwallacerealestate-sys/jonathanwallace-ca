Coles Notes: Common Conditions and Warranties in an Ontario Offer

By Jonathan Wallace, REALTOR®, Faris Team Real Estate Brokerage · Updated November 2026 · ~11 min read

Thinking of selling? Get Your Personalized Home Value Report

An offer on a home is more than a price and a closing date. Most of the protection lives on a page called Schedule A, where the extra terms are written. Those terms come in three types. Once you know the difference, an offer gets a lot easier to read.

A condition is an "I'll do this deal if..." clause. It gives the buyer, or sometimes the seller, a set amount of time to check something before the deal is final. Think of it like agreeing to buy a used car as long as your mechanic gives it a clean bill of health. If you're not satisfied, and you say so in writing before the deadline, you can walk away and get your deposit back.

Deadlines are counted carefully. If a condition gives you five business days after the offer is accepted, the day it's accepted doesn't count. Day one is the next business day, and weekends and holidays are skipped. So an offer accepted on a Friday starts counting on Monday.

A representation is the seller saying "this is true." A warranty is the seller promising "I'll stand behind it." Say the seller promises the dishwasher will be in good working order on closing day. If it dies 10 days before closing, the seller has to repair or replace it at their own expense. Once you have the keys, the promise is done. It's not an extended warranty, so if the dishwasher breaks in March, that's on you.

A disclosure is information. The seller tells you something, like "the basement leaked in 2019 and we fixed it." That's useful to know, but it isn't a promise, and on its own it doesn't give you a way out of the deal. Sellers do have to tell you about serious hidden problems they know about, the kind that make a home unsafe or unlivable.

This is a plain-English guide, not legal advice. Every deal is different, so have your REALTOR® and your lawyer look at your actual offer.

Balance of purchase price

This one is simple. Your deposit goes in first, when the offer is accepted. The rest of the price, called the balance, gets paid on closing day.

You don't hand that money to the seller yourself. Your lawyer gathers your mortgage money and the rest of your savings, then sends it to the seller's lawyer by bank draft, certified cheque or a secure bank-to-bank wire. No personal cheques.

One tip: the price isn't the only money you need on closing day. Budget separately for your lawyer, land transfer tax and other closing costs.

Home inspection and insurance

These two usually travel together. They give you time to check the house before you're locked in.

The inspection condition lets you have the home inspected and walk away if you're not happy with what you learn. The wording I use doesn't say who has to do the inspection. A professional home inspector is the right call for most homes, but a builder or contractor you trust can do the walk-through instead. What matters is that you're satisfied, and that you say so in writing before the deadline.

Insurance is the one people forget. Just because the seller has insurance doesn't mean you'll get it at a price you like. Get a real quote on this house. You might budget $100 a month and get quoted $380, because of an old wood stove, older wiring or a history of claims. The condition gives you time to find that out before you're committed.

One thing an inspection condition doesn't do is force the seller to fix anything. If the inspection turns something up, you can ask the seller to fix it or lower the price, or walk away before the deadline.

Financing

A financing condition gives you time to get your mortgage approved for this specific house. If the bank says no, you can walk away and get your deposit back, as long as you give notice before the deadline.

A pre-approval is not an approval. Think of a pre-approval as the bank saying "you look good on paper." The real approval comes after the bank looks at the actual house, its value and condition, and checks that nothing has changed with you.

That last part matters. Don't buy a new car, change jobs or move your down payment around between pre-approval and closing. Any of those can change the bank's answer. Before you remove this condition, make sure the bank has approved this house, not just you.

Sale of the buyer's property, escape clause, and pause clause

If you need to sell your current home before you can buy, you can make your offer conditional on that sale. Sellers usually give you about 30 to 60 days to sell. That's negotiated, not automatic.

Sellers often add an escape clause to protect themselves. It lets them keep the house on the market. If another buyer comes along, they let you know, and you usually get 24 to 48 hours to decide: remove your condition and commit, or step aside and get your deposit back.

That's where the pause clause comes in, and most buyers have never heard of it. Picture this. You've just accepted an offer on your own home, but your buyer still has a week to do their inspection. Then you get an escape notice on the home you're buying. Without a pause, you're stuck choosing between committing before your own sale is final, or losing the house and risking your buyer going firm while you have nowhere to go.

A pause clause freezes the escape clause for a short, agreed time, often about seven business days, so your own sale has time to firm up. Both sides have to agree to it in writing. Some versions start the pause when you receive an offer on your home, others when you accept one, so know which one you have.

Seller finding suitable accommodation

This one protects sellers, and it's underused. It says: I'll sell you my home at this price, as long as I can find my next place to live by a certain date.

It takes the fear out of selling first. You lock in your sale price, then shop knowing exactly what you have to work with. If you can't find a place you're happy with by the deadline, the deal ends and the buyer gets their deposit back.

Buyers have to agree to it, because it leaves them waiting. In the right situation, it's a fair middle ground.

Septic warranty

If the house isn't on town sewer, it has a septic system: a tank in the yard and a bed of pipes that drains into the ground. Fixing or replacing one is expensive, so the offer usually includes a septic promise.

The seller promises, as far as they know, that the system was installed with the right permits, has been looked after, and will be working on closing day. The seller also usually agrees to have the tank pumped and inspected before closing and to hand over the paperwork.

My advice to sellers: pump and inspect when you list, not after you sell. It's like getting your car checked before you sell it. You find out about a cracked tank or a failing bed before you've promised it works.

Buyers: a pump-out report is helpful, but it doesn't check everything. A separate septic inspection is still worth it.

Well and water warranty

If the house gets its water from a well, there are two questions: is there enough water, and is it safe to drink? They're different questions.

The seller usually promises that while they've lived there, the well has supplied enough water for normal use, like showers, dishes and laundry, and that there's no sign of contamination. If the seller knows two loads of laundry runs the well dry, they need to say so.

The clause usually lets you take a water sample before closing. If the result isn't acceptable, the seller fixes it at their cost. Ontario's free well water test checks for bacteria, like E. coli. It isn't a full chemical test.

Test early. Many lenders want a clean water test before they'll release the mortgage money.

Chattels, fixtures, and what the warranty covers

Here's an easy way to remember the difference. If you could turn the house upside down and it would fall out, it's a chattel, like a couch or a freestanding fridge. If it's attached, it's a fixture, like a light fixture or built-in shelves. Fixtures normally stay with the house.

If a seller wants to take something attached, it has to be written into the offer before anyone signs. Say the dining-room chandelier was a wedding gift. Sentimental value doesn't keep it. It has to be listed as excluded, or it stays.

Most offers also include a promise that the appliances and the home's systems, like heating, plumbing and electrical, will be working on closing day. If the dishwasher dies ten days before closing, the seller has to repair or replace it at their own expense before you get the keys.

That promise is about closing day, not forever. That's why your last walk-through before closing matters. It's your chance to check that everything works.

Sellers: if you know something isn't working, say so and write an exception into the offer. Don't promise it works.

Stigmas versus defects

A stigma is something about a home's history that might bother a buyer even though nothing is physically wrong, like a death in the home or its past use as a grow-op. A defect is different. It's a physical problem with the house.

Sellers must tell buyers about serious hidden defects they know about. Stigmas are treated differently. A seller doesn't always have to bring them up. If you ask, they can decline to answer, but they can't lie.

I usually recommend a stigma clause, where the seller promises that, as far as they know, there has never been a murder, death or suicide in the home. It protects your peace of mind and your resale down the road. If something specific matters to you, ask about it directly and get the answer in writing.

Condo status certificate

When you buy a condo, you're buying your unit plus a share of the whole building. The status certificate is the building's report card: its finances, its savings for future repairs, any special fees coming up, and its rules.

I recommend making your offer conditional on your lawyer reviewing it. In the offers I write, the seller orders it, the building has 10 days to produce it, and your lawyer then gets a few business days to go through it with you.

Your lawyer is looking for things like a thin repair fund or a big special fee on the way, the kind of surprise that can cost you thousands after you move in. Keep in mind it checks the paperwork, not the building itself.

HST: included in the price, or in addition?

Most resale homes don't have HST. The offer form still asks whether HST, if it applies, is included in the price or added on top.

Where it can matter is vacant land, new or heavily renovated homes, and property sold by a business. If you're buying or selling land, a severed lot or a property owned by a company, talk to an accountant before you sign.

The deposit

The deposit is your good-faith money. It shows the seller you're serious. It's usually held in trust by the listing brokerage and goes toward your purchase price on closing day.

If the offer says "upon acceptance," you have 24 hours after the offer is accepted to deliver it. That's true even when the offer still has conditions.

Plan ahead. Know how your bank does drafts or wires, and confirm wire details by phone before you send money. If you need more time, negotiate it before you sign. A late deposit can put the whole deal at risk.

Buyer visits

Most offers I write give the buyer two visits before closing, not counting the inspection and the appraisal. They're usually about an hour each, at a time that works for the seller, with your agent there.

Use the first visit to measure for furniture, pick paint colours or bring family to see the place. Let the seller know who's coming.

Save the second visit for a day or two before closing. That's your final walk-through: check that everything included in the sale is there and working. Expect boxes. Packing mess is normal.

Survey if available

A survey is a drawing that shows where your property lines are and where the house, garage and fences sit.

The usual clause only asks the seller to hand over a survey if they already have one. It doesn't make them pay for a new one. Keep in mind an old survey may not show newer additions, decks or fences.

Water seepage, leaks, and flooding

A basement can look bone dry in July and take on water every April. That's why I like to include a water damage clause.

In it, the seller promises that, as far as they know, there's no damage from water leaks or flooding in the basement, the roof or anywhere else on closing day. It puts the question on paper instead of trusting a dry showing.

Also ask directly: has it ever leaked, does it leak every spring, and has anything been repaired? Ask for the repair records if there are any.

Bottom line

Think of Schedule A like checking the map before a winter drive up the shore. Conditions give you time. Warranties are promises about closing day. Disclosures are information. Deadlines matter. Make the wording fit the home, and lean on your REALTOR®, lawyer, lender and inspectors when you need them.
