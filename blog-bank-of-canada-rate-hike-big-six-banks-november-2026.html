Did the Bank of Canada Raise Rates Too Soon? What Canada's Big Six Banks Are Saying

By Jonathan Wallace, REALTOR®, Faris Team Real Estate Brokerage · Updated November 2026 · ~7 min read

Thinking of selling? Book a home evaluation

The short answer: On October 28, 2026, the Bank of Canada raised its key interest rate by a quarter point to 2.50%, its first increase since 2023. Economists at Canada's six biggest banks are split on whether it was the right call. Scotiabank and National Bank's rate strategists backed the move. RBC, TD and CIBC called it reasonable insurance but felt there was a strong case for waiting. National Bank's chief economist and a senior BMO investment executive said it came too early. Almost all of them expect at least one more increase, most likely by early 2027.

Where each bank stands
  • Scotiabank: supports the hike. Expects another in December and a possible peak of 3.25% by mid-2027.
  • National Bank: split. Its rate strategists supported the hike and lean toward December. Chief economist Stéfane Marion said core inflation called for patience.
  • RBC: a "risk management hike." Expects another increase in December or January.
  • TD: had called for a hold. Expects one more hike in early 2027, to 2.75%.
  • CIBC: questioned whether any hikes were needed. Expects a January hike to 2.75%, then a long pause.
  • BMO: Earl Davis of BMO Global Asset Management called it "premature."

What the Bank of Canada Did, and Why

The Bank raised its policy rate from 2.25% to 2.50%. Its reasoning was about where inflation is heading, not where it is today.

  1. Inflation is now expected to run higher for longer. The Bank expects inflation to stay above 3% through much of 2027. In July, it expected about 2%.
  2. Energy is the main worry. Oil prices are expected to stay high, and the Bank wants to stop those costs from spreading into the price of everything else.
  3. It left the door open to more. The Bank said there "could be a need to increase the policy rate further," but that it would "proceed carefully." Governor Tiff Macklem said there is "no predetermined path."

Macklem was candid about the impact on households: "It's not going to feel good immediately, but that's how you get inflation back down."

The next decision is December 9, 2026.

The Case for Raising Rates Now

  1. Scotiabank: act early to avoid acting harder later. Scotiabank's Derek Holt titled his note "Kudos to the Bank of Canada." He argued the Bank is "not dithering as it did in the pandemic," when it waited too long and then had to raise rates sharply. Scotiabank expects another hike in December and sees rates possibly reaching 3.25% by mid-2027.
  2. National Bank's rate strategists: the inflation outlook changed. Taylor Schleich and Ethan Currie wrote that the Bank made "a fundamental rethink on the inflation outlook," and with price stability not expected soon, "it's hard to make the case to go slowly." They lean toward another hike in December.
  3. RBC: a sensible insurance policy. Before the decision, RBC had "very low conviction" that a hike would come this soon. Afterward, chief economist Frances Donald and Nathan Janzen called it "essentially a risk management hike" and said "the economy isn't likely to be knocked off course by this or a few more rate hikes." They expect another increase in December or January.

The Case That It Came Too Soon

  1. National Bank's chief economist: core inflation does not justify it. Stéfane Marion argued the core data called for patience, pointing out that "core inflation is still at 2%," right on the Bank's target.
  2. BMO: premature. Earl Davis, head of fixed income and money markets at BMO Global Asset Management, called the move "premature" and "pre-emptive." He allowed it may prove right in hindsight, but expects markets to scale back how many more hikes they are betting on.
  3. TD: patience was the better option. TD had called for a hold. Afterward, economist Marc Ercolao wrote, "We felt the case for patience was strong based on the latest data." TD accepts the Bank's inflation concerns and now expects one more increase in early 2027, to 2.75%, then a much higher bar for anything beyond that.
  4. CIBC: energy is the only real pressure. Before the decision, chief economist Avery Shenfeld wrote, "We aren't yet sure that ANY rate hikes will be warranted in the coming year," and argued waiting would give the Bank more time to judge oil prices and U.S. trade talks. CIBC now expects a hold in December, a hike in January to 2.75%, and then a long pause, because a soft job market should keep core inflation in check.

Where the Banks Agree

For all the debate about timing, the forecasts are closer than the headlines suggest.

  1. More hikes are likely. Every bank expects at least one more quarter-point increase. The main disagreement is whether it comes in December or early 2027.
  2. The peak is still modest. Forecasts for where rates top out range from about 2.75% (TD, CIBC) to 3.25% (Scotiabank). For comparison, the Bank's rate peaked at 5% in 2023.
  3. Oil and trade will decide the rest. A drop in oil prices or a serious escalation in the Canada-U.S. trade dispute could slow or stop further increases.

What This Means for Georgian Bay Buyers, Sellers and Homeowners

  1. Variable-rate borrowers are paying more already. The big banks raised their prime rate after the decision. As a rough guide, each quarter-point increase adds about $70 a month on a $500,000 mortgage with a 25-year amortization.
  2. Plan for one or two more increases. Whichever bank turns out to be right, budgeting for rates a half point higher than today covers most forecasts.
  3. Buyers: get a rate hold. Most lenders will hold a pre-approval rate for 90 to 120 days. If rates rise again in December or January, you are protected. If you are weighing rate types, see Fixed or Variable? What This Fall's Mortgage Rate Gap Means for Georgian Bay Buyers.
  4. Renewing in 2027: start early. Talk to your lender or a mortgage professional four to six months before renewal so you can compare options instead of accepting the first offer.
  5. Sellers: price to today's buyers. Higher borrowing costs make buyers more careful. A home priced to recent local sales in Midland, Penetanguishene, Tiny, Tay or Wasaga Beach will draw attention. One priced on last year's numbers will sit. A free home value estimate is a good first step.

Questions People Ask

Why did the Bank of Canada raise interest rates in October 2026?

The Bank raised its key rate to 2.50% on October 28, 2026 because it now expects inflation to stay above 3% through much of 2027, driven mainly by high energy prices. It wants to stop those costs from spreading into other prices.

Do Canada's big banks agree with the rate hike?

They are split. Scotiabank and National Bank's rate strategists supported it. RBC, TD and CIBC called it a reasonable risk management move but saw a strong case for waiting. National Bank's chief economist and a BMO investment executive said it came too early.

Will the Bank of Canada raise rates again in December 2026?

Possibly. Scotiabank and National Bank's strategists lean toward a December hike. CIBC and TD expect the next increase in early 2027 instead. The decision is December 9, 2026.

How high will Canadian interest rates go?

Bank forecasts range from about 2.75% (TD and CIBC) to about 3.25% (Scotiabank) by 2027. That is well below the 5% peak in 2023.

What does the rate hike mean for my mortgage?

Variable-rate payments rise with the prime rate, roughly $70 a month per quarter point on a $500,000 mortgage. Fixed-rate payments do not change until renewal, but new fixed rates depend on bond markets, which already expect more hikes.

All answers are current as of November 3, 2026. Bank views are summarized from their published commentary and media interviews.
