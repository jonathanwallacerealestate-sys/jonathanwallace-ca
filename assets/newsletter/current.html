The offer that depends on another sale

Those two numbers explain it. When homes take 47 days to move, nobody wants to sell first and then rent until spring hoping the right house appears. So instead of selling and then buying, people find the house they want and write the offer with a condition attached: this becomes real once my own home sells.

On the board it shows as sold conditionally, or where the seller keeps marketing the home, sold conditionally with an escape clause. The escape clause is the part most people misunderstand. It means the home stays listed. If a second offer comes in, the first buyer gets notice, commonly 24 to 72 hours, and either drops the condition or steps aside. The exact window is whatever the agreement says, not what someone told you once.

If you are the seller

The instinct is to say no, and the instinct is usually wrong. The buyer who has to sell first is very often the buyer who wants your house most. Usually local, usually moving up or downsizing, not shopping four towns at once. That tends to show up in the number, and a conditional offer at a price you would happily take beats a firm offer at a price that stings.

The real cost is attention, not the deposit. A listing does its heaviest work in its first two or three weeks, and plenty of buyers filter out anything that reads as sold without ever checking for an escape clause. So in week one it is an expensive yes. By week six, when fresh traffic has thinned, it is a reasonable one. Past sixty days, a live buyer beats another quiet weekend. Then stop reading their offer and go look at their house. Is it listed yet. Is it priced against what has actually sold on that street. How long are comparable homes there sitting. And is their own buyer conditional too, which quietly puts three households in a chain.

If you are the buyer

The condition keeps you from owning two homes at once, which is worth having. It also makes you the weaker offer, and you will pay for that somewhere, in price, in terms, or in losing a house that has other interest. Under an escape clause you can be knocked out by someone who showed up after you did, with a day or two to decide whether to firm up while your own home is still unsold. That is where people waive protections they actually needed.

So do the work first. List your own home and price it properly, because an unlisted home is not a plan, it is an intention, and every seller can tell the difference. Sort out bridge financing before you need it, so you can say yes when the notice comes. And set the condition against evidence rather than optimism: if homes like yours take seventy days, a thirty day condition is designed to expire.

Where the two sides meet

Most of these offers can be countered into something workable. Insist on the escape clause. Tie the condition to a home that is genuinely listed at an agreed price by a set date. Keep the period short and matched to what that segment is really taking. And make sure you ask your realtor about how the escape clause affects you, because the notice period, how the days are counted, and what happens to the deposit all live in the wording.
