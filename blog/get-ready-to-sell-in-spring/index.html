Getting Ready to Sell in Spring: Your Fall-to-Spring Checklist for Georgian Bay Homes

By Jonathan Wallace, Realtor with Faris Team Real Estate Brokerage

Midland · Penetanguishene · Tiny · Tay · Wasaga Beach · ~12 min read

Download the printable checklist (PDF) What's my home worth?
If you're planning to sell in the spring, start now. The best spring listings I've worked on around Georgian Bay were put together the fall before. The outside work has to be done before the ground freezes, the inside work fills the winter, and by March you're pricing and booking trades, not scrambling.

Most people think about selling in the spring and start getting ready in April. By then the lot is wet, the snowbanks are grey, and every contractor in North Simcoe is booked. Selling isn't really about a fresh coat of paint. It's about taking away every reason a buyer has to hesitate or knock money off your price. That takes time, and in our part of Ontario, the weather decides what you can do and when.

Here's how I'd lay it out, month by month, plus the room-by-room list, what's worth fixing (and what isn't), and the paperwork that makes rural and waterfront homes easier to sell.

Do this first

Take your photos now, while the property looks its best

This is the one I tell every fall client. In April, exteriors look drab, the lot is wet, nothing is growing and the trees are bare. Right now your gardens, lawn, trees and the water look the way you want buyers to picture them.

Get your exterior photos (and drone shots, if your property calls for them) done while the colour is still up. We'll take fresh interior photos right before you list, and use your fall exteriors alongside them. Buyers see the property at its best, not at its muddiest.

Real estate photographers recommend the same thing for off-season listings: Aryeo, Steamboat Springs Real Estate.

Your fall-to-spring timeline

October to November · before the freeze

Outside work and the big unknowns

  • Fall exterior and drone photos. (See above.) If you have waterfront, photograph the shoreline and the dock before they come out or freeze in.
  • Book a pre-listing home inspection. This is the big one. An inspection now means you find the deficiencies on your schedule, not the buyer's, and you have all winter to deal with them. The things you can't fix, you can price for or disclose up front.
  • Get the septic pumped and inspected. Keep the hauler's written report. If the inside of the tank needs parging, it has to be done while temperatures are still holding above about 10°C so the cement cures properly. Check the product's own temperature requirements with your contractor. Once November turns cold, that job waits until spring, when you'd rather be listing. In Tiny, also check whether your area is part of the Township's septic re-inspection program and whether you have any open deficiency notices.
  • Test your well water. Simcoe Muskoka District Health Unit provides free bacteria testing. Pick up bottles at the Midland office, and the sample has to be tested within 48 hours (SMDHU). You'll test again after the spring melt.
  • Fix loose handrails on the deck and any wobbly steps. It's a small job, and every inspector flags it. Buyers notice right away when a railing moves under their hand.
  • Open up the attic and check for unexpected visitors. Look for droppings, nesting, chewed wiring and disturbed insulation. There's nothing worse than the buyer's inspector finding a rodent problem. In my experience, about half of buyers are instantly turned off when that happens, and the rest start second-guessing their decision. If you find something, deal with it now and keep the pest-control invoice.
  • Service the furnace and A/C if it's not too late in the season, and keep the records. It shows buyers everything has been maintained and is working.
  • Clean the gutters and winterize outdoor taps and hoses (CMHC maintenance schedule). After the first light snow, look for spots where the snow on the roof melts first. They show heat leaking into the attic, which is where ice dams start (NRCan).
  • Book a WETT inspection for any wood stove, insert or pellet stove. Many insurers require one, and if a buyer can't get insurance, they can't get financing (WETT Inc.).
  • Start any shoreline approvals now. Docks, retaining walls and beach work can need Township, conservation authority or other approvals, and none of them happen fast. See Tiny's shoreline construction page and the NVCA permit FAQ.

December to January · inside work

Work through the inspection list

  • Get rid of water stains. Here's a common one: a slow leak got fixed properly by a professional four years ago, but nobody ever painted the ceiling. Now's the time. Prime it with a stain blocker, paint it, and put the repair invoice in your house book. You're taking away the question before a buyer can ask it. (A known past issue still gets disclosed. The paint just means nobody has to wonder whether it's still leaking.)
  • Declutter room by room. Pack up what you won't need until you move. Winter is the perfect time for it.
  • Finish the small repairs: dripping taps, sticky doors, cracked caulking, burnt-out bulbs, missing cover plates.
  • Test smoke and CO alarms. You need a CO alarm on every storey and outside sleeping areas if you have a fuel-burning appliance, a fireplace or an attached garage (Ontario.ca).
  • Start your house book (more below) and gather receipts, warranties and permits for any renovations.
  • If the place sits empty, read your insurance policy's vacancy and heating clauses. Frozen-pipe damage in a vacant home is often not covered (IBC).
  • Consider a winter radon test. A long-term test runs at least 91 days, so starting in winter means you'll have results by spring (Health Canada).

February to March · paperwork and pricing

Get the documents and the number ready

  • Find your survey. If it's old or doesn't show your current fences, additions or outbuildings, talk to me about whether an updated Surveyor's Real Property Report makes sense (AOLS).
  • Order record searches. In Tiny, you can order a septic record search for your permit history (Tiny). The ESA can tell you if there are open electrical notifications on the property, and its full history requests take about 15 business days (ESA).
  • Oil tank? Find its age, location and inspection records. Older tanks and tanks of unknown age can create insurance and legal issues, so get the facts from TSSA or a registered contractor early (O. Reg. 213/01).
  • Private road or right-of-way? Gather any road-maintenance or snowplowing agreements. Buyers and lenders will want to know how year-round access works.
  • Talk to me about disclosure. Make a list of anything you know about. Sellers in Ontario have to disclose latent defects (RECO), and it's much easier to handle up front.
  • Review pricing. In late winter, we'll go over a fresh comparative market analysis based on what's sold and what you'll be competing with.
  • Book your photographer, cleaners, stager and trades. Spring is their busy season too.

The final 2 to 4 weeks before you list

Spring walk-around, deep clean, photos

  • Walk the property and check the roof, gutters, foundation, grading and sump pump for winter damage.
  • Re-test the well after the spring melt, and if you have a cottage, open it up carefully: restore the water, check for burst lines and test the pressure tank.
  • Deep clean everything: windows, baseboards, light fixtures, grout, the inside of the appliances. A well-cared-for house sells for more. Buyers read a clean house as a well-maintained house.
  • Depersonalize and stage the main rooms, and clear the walkways of snow, ice and slush.
  • Interior photos on a bright day, paired with your fall exteriors.
  • Put the house book on the kitchen counter.

Get ahead of the negotiation

Can't afford a fix? Get three quotes before you list

Sometimes the inspection turns up something that needs replacing (the roof, the septic bed, the windows), and the money just isn't there to do it before you sell. That's okay. What isn't okay is letting the buyer set the price of the repair.

Buyers always inflate the cost. You'll hear "that's going to cost $20K to fix" across the table, and it becomes their number for knocking down your price. Get three written quotes from reputable local contractors before you list. Then, when it comes up, you can say, "I have three quotes, and they're all around $11K." Now you're negotiating from facts, not from somebody's guess.

Get ahead of it before it gets used as leverage. Put the quotes in your house book.

My favourite tip

Make a house book

A house book is a binder that tells the story of how your home has been looked after. Buyers eat this up. Every question it answers before they ask is one less objection later, and fewer objections means a smoother deal and a stronger price.

What goes in it:

  • Service records: furnace, A/C, septic pump-outs, well tests, chimney/WETT
  • A list of what's been updated and when (roof, windows, furnace, water heater, electrical, plumbing)
  • Receipts and invoices for repairs and renovations, plus any permits
  • Warranties and appliance manuals
  • Your three quotes for anything you didn't fix
  • Utility and heating costs for the past year

Here's why it matters so much. A skilled agent can walk through a house and tell you roughly how old the furnace, the roof and the windows are, because we've seen it all. A less-skilled agent just opens the door, and the buyer is left guessing. Guessing usually means assuming the worst. Your house book answers those questions no matter who shows the home.

Have it ready to leave out on the kitchen counter for every showing.

Optional, if you're tech-savvy: scan everything into a shared Google Drive folder and print a QR code on the cover that links to it. Buyers can look through the records right on their phones.

What's worth fixing, and what to think twice about

Before you spend money, ask yourself: will a buyer notice this, and will it make them hesitate? If the answer is no, put the money back in your pocket.

Room-by-room checklist

Entry and exterior

  • Front door clean, hardware working, light fixture working
  • Deck railings and steps solid
  • Gutters clear, downspouts pointing away from the house
  • Snow and ice cleared from walkways for showings

Kitchen

  • Counters cleared to the essentials
  • Appliances cleaned inside and out
  • Cabinet doors aligned, hinges tightened
  • House book on the counter

Bathrooms

  • Caulking redone where it's cracked or stained
  • No dripping taps or running toilets
  • Fan working
  • Fresh towels, personal items put away

Living areas and bedrooms

  • Decluttered and depersonalized
  • Scuffs and ceiling stains painted
  • Every bulb working, closets half empty

Basement, attic and mechanical

  • Attic checked for rodents and wildlife
  • Furnace and A/C serviced, filter changed
  • Sump pump tested; no dampness or musty smell
  • Smoke and CO alarms tested

Rural, cottage and waterfront

  • Septic pumped and inspected, report on file; leaching bed clear of vehicles, patios and fire pits (Tiny)
  • Recent well water results
  • WETT certificate for any wood stove
  • Survey, shoreline and dock permits on file
  • Road or snowplowing agreements if you're on a private road

Before the photographer arrives

  • Every light on, every bulb the same colour
  • Blinds open, windows clean
  • Counters, fridge door and bathroom vanities cleared
  • Cars out of the driveway, garbage bins out of sight
  • Beds made, towels folded, toilet seats down
  • Pets and pet bowls out of the shot
  • Snow cleared from walkways and the deck if it's still around

Frequently asked questions

When should I start getting my house ready to sell in the spring?

Start in October. Around Georgian Bay, the outside jobs (fall photos, septic, deck repairs, anything involving cement) have to happen before the freeze. Winter is for the inside work and the paperwork, and February and March are for pricing and booking your photographer and trades. If you wait until April, you're doing everything at once, in the mud.

Should I take my listing photos in the fall if I'm selling in the spring?

Yes, at least the exterior shots. In April, exteriors look drab, the lot is wet, nothing is growing and the trees are bare. Fall photos of your yard, gardens and water in colour can sit alongside fresh interior photos taken right before you list.

Is a pre-listing home inspection worth it?

In my opinion, yes, especially if you're listing in the spring. An inspection now gives you the whole winter to fix, price for or disclose what it finds, instead of hearing about it for the first time from the buyer's inspector, when it gets used to negotiate your price down.

Do I need to pump and inspect my septic before I sell?

I'd do it before you list. Buyers will ask for it, and most offers on septic properties call for it anyway. Do it in the fall while the ground is open, and keep the hauler's written report for your house book. If the tank needs parging inside, that has to happen while it's still warm enough for the cement to cure properly.

Should I paint over an old water stain if the leak was fixed years ago?

Yes. Fix the cause first, then prime and paint so the stain is gone, and keep the repair invoice in your house book. Painting doesn't replace disclosing a known issue. What it does is take away the buyer's "is this still leaking?" question. When they ask, you hand them the invoice.

What if something needs replacing and I can't afford to do it before I sell?

Get three written quotes before you list. Buyers almost always guess high, and that guess becomes their negotiating number. With three quotes in hand, you can answer with real numbers instead of arguing about a guess.

What is a house book, and do buyers actually look at it?

It's a binder (or a shared folder) with your service records, receipts, invoices, warranties, manuals and a list of what's been updated and when. Buyers do read it. Leave it on the kitchen counter for showings. Every question it answers is one less objection later.

What extra paperwork do I need to sell a cottage or rural home on Georgian Bay?

Usually a septic pump-out report and permit history, a recent well water test, a WETT inspection for any wood stove, information on any oil tank, your survey, and any shoreline or dock permits. If you have private-road access, you'll need your road or snowplowing agreement too. Start gathering these in the fall. Some take weeks.
