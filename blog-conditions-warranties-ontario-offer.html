Coles Notes: Common Conditions and Warranties in an Ontario Offer

By Jonathan Wallace, REALTOR®, Faris Team Real Estate Brokerage · Updated November 2026 · ~14 min read

Thinking of selling? Book a home evaluation

Offers around Georgian Bay are rarely just a price and a closing date. Schedule A can set out conditions, representations, warranties and disclosures. Conditions set out what needs to happen, by when, and what happens if it does not. Representations and warranties record statements and promises. Disclosures give the buyer information about the property.

This is a general Ontario explainer. It is not an interpretation of any one signed contract. Work with your REALTOR® and your lawyer on the wording that fits your property.

A condition gives the buyer or seller it protects an opportunity to satisfy themselves about the terms spelled out in that condition, within the timeframe both sides agree to. A buyer might need to be satisfied with financing or an inspection. A seller might need to find suitable accommodation. The clause sets the deadline and exactly what written notice must be delivered, to whom and how. Missing those requirements can end the agreement, with the deposit dealt with as the clause says.

Where a condition counts a number of business days after acceptance, day one is the first business day after an offer is accepted. The acceptance day does not count. Skip weekends and statutory holidays as the clause defines them. Some conditions instead use a fixed date or start after something else happens, such as the lawyer receiving a status certificate. Always check the actual clause and its deadline time.

A representation is the seller saying, “This is true.” A warranty is the seller promising, “I’ll stand behind this.”

For example, if the seller promises the dishwasher will work on closing day, and the agreement says that promise continues after closing, handing over the keys doesn’t erase that promise. If you discover afterward that it was already broken when you took possession, the seller may still be responsible. But if it was working on closing day and breaks later, that promise doesn’t automatically make the seller responsible.

A disclosure tells the buyer about a fact or situation, such as a known leak or a previous repair. A seller can provide that information separately or in the agreement. A clause saying the seller “represents and warrants” something also puts a statement and a promise into the contract. That is not the same as simply sharing information, and a disclosure does not automatically become a warranty. What the buyer can do with that information depends on the agreement and the circumstances. It does not, by itself, set a deadline or give either side a right to walk away. Sellers also have important duties to disclose known hidden defects that make a property unfit to live in, dangerous or potentially dangerous. Stigmas are treated differently.

Balance of purchase price

This is the first clause in the Schedule A I typically use. After the deposit is credited, the buyer still owes the balance of the purchase price, subject to usual closing adjustments. That money is paid to the seller on completion through the lawyers, with funds drawn on a lawyer's trust account.

Accepted forms in the wording I commonly use are a bank draft, a certified cheque, or a wire transfer using the Lynx high value payment system under the Canadian Payments Act. That is the high-value electronic lawyer-to-lawyer path, not a personal cheque at the kitchen table.

Keep two piles separate: the balance of the purchase price, and your own closing costs and lawyer disbursements. Your lawyer usually brings the mortgage advance together with your remaining cash so trust funds can be paid in an accepted form.

Home inspection and insurance

In many of the offers I write, inspection and insurance travel together. The condition period is time for independent advice before the deal goes firm.

The home inspection clause I use makes the offer conditional on the buyer arranging a home inspection satisfactory to the buyer, in the buyer's sole and absolute discretion. Written notice is due by the deadline. If that notice is not given on time, the offer can end and the deposit comes back in full without deduction. The seller must co-operate on access. The buyer may waive in writing.

That wording does not say who has to do the inspection. A professional home inspector is the right call for most homes, and a full written report is worth having. But a builder, a contractor or a knowledgeable friend you trust can do the walk-through instead. What matters is that you are satisfied, and that you give written notice before the deadline.

Insurance is usually added for a reason. Do not assume the seller having a policy means you will get an affordable or acceptable one. Get a formal quote on this property. Hypothetical example only: you budget about $100 a month, and the quote comes back around $380. Flood exposure, previous claims, knob-and-tube wiring, or a wood-burning fireplace can raise the premium, add requirements, or make coverage harder to place. If the insurance condition is in the agreement and the quote is not satisfactory, the notice and deposit rules in that clause govern. Rural and waterfront offers may also bundle well, septic, and WETT checks. An inspection condition does not, by itself, force repairs.

Financing

A financing condition is about arranging financing satisfactory to the buyer within the time and notice rules in the agreement. It is usually for the buyer's benefit and can be waived in writing. It is not a guarantee that the lender will fund.

Pre-approval is not final, property-specific approval. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada is clear that pre-approval does not guarantee a mortgage. It may hold a rate for a lender-set period and indicate an amount you might qualify to borrow. The lender still underwrites the specific home, including things like property taxes, value, condition, and use under that lender's guidelines, plus your current finances and any conditions of approval. Different lenders and products set different thresholds.

Things can change between pre-approval and funding: a new car loan, fewer hours or lower pay, or less down payment and liquid funds than when you first qualified. Not every asset or loan file is treated the same way. Before you waive financing, confirm what is approved for this property, what conditions remain, and keep financing in place until the lender's closing conditions are met. Even a commitment is not a universal guarantee of funding if conditions are missed or material facts change.

Sale of the buyer's property, escape clause, and pause clause

If you still own a home, the offer may be conditional on that home selling by a fixed date and time. In the deals I negotiate, sellers often grant about 30 to 60 days. That is negotiated, not automatic.

I usually stack an escape clause. In my practice the notice window is often 24 or 48 hours. The seller can keep marketing. If another acceptable offer arrives, the signed clause says how notice works. On wording I commonly use, the first buyer then has a short window to waive any and all conditions in writing or the offer ends and the deposit comes back without deduction. A second offer does not automatically cancel the first outside that notice path.

The piece buyers forget is the pause clause, the "do not end up without a next house" protection. Hypothetical example only: you accept an offer on your own home with about seven banking days for inspection. During that window you get a 48-hour escape notice on the home you are buying. Without a pause, you may have to firm up before your own sale is firm, or walk away and then watch your buyer firm up while you have nowhere to go.

A pause is a limited, agreed window after you have an accepted conditional sale on your own property. In my practice that stretch is often about seven business or banking days, and it is negotiable. Both sides must agree in writing. It does not automatically cover every conditional period or guarantee housing.

Be precise about the trigger. Some schedules pause the escape when you receive an offer on your home with a conditional period no longer than an agreed maximum. Other wording, and the practice I prefer to explain, protects you once you accept an offer on your own home. The signed trigger governs. Receiving an offer and accepting one are not the same thing.

Seller finding suitable accommodation

This is an underused seller strategy in my practice. I will sell you my home at this agreed price if you give me a negotiated number of days to secure satisfactory accommodation first.

"Suitable" follows the signed wording. Office clause language often makes the offer conditional on the seller finding accommodation satisfactory to the seller, sometimes in the seller's sole and absolute discretion, with written notice by a set deadline. That next place may be a purchase, a rental, or short-term lodging if the wording allows. If the required notice is not given on time, the agreement ends under that clause and the deposit returns to the buyer without deduction. Discretion still sits inside the contract and ordinary good-faith expectations.

Sellers like it because they can agree a sale price first, then shop with a clearer picture of what they are working with. It does not guarantee net proceeds or final budget, and the sale is still conditional until the condition is fulfilled or waived. Buyers have to agree, because they take uncertainty. Used carefully, it is a balanced option for the right seller, not automatically the best move in every market.

Septic warranty

On rural Georgian Bay deals, septic language shows up for good reason. A typical clause has two jobs: promises about the system, and a seller-paid pump and inspect by a set date with paperwork.

In wording I commonly use, the seller represents and warrants, to the best of the seller's knowledge and belief, about matters at the time of installation such as setbacks, certificates, and use permits. The same clause also speaks to maintenance during the seller's occupancy and working order on closing. Read those pieces separately. The signed words decide what is qualified.

The seller often agrees, at their expense, to have the tank pumped and inspected by a professional and to provide a receipt by the title-search date. A recent pump-out with documents can sometimes satisfy the pump-out piece. Survival language, when included, usually applies only to the state of the property at completion.

My advice to sellers: pump and inspect when you list, so you know what is underground before you promise working order. Pump companies often leave a report-card style note on age, cracking, inlet and outlet, tees, and lids. General examples of bad news include a cracked concrete tank, a failing tile bed, or crumbling lids. Those findings can mean expensive repair or replacement.

A standard pump-out report is useful. It may not fully assess the tile bed or the entire system, and it does not guarantee future performance. A separate buyer septic inspection condition is still useful. A problem under a warranty does not automatically kill the deal or return the deposit by itself. What happens next follows the contract: recommended scope, permits where required, and a written repair, replace, credit, or other resolution.

Well and water warranty

Water quantity and water quality are different questions.

In wording I commonly use, the seller represents and warrants that during their occupancy the well pump and related equipment performed adequately, that the well can supply water sufficient for normal household use, and that there is no evidence of contamination. Some offers soften that with a knowledge qualifier. Others do not. Read yours.

In everyday terms, adequate supply means you can run a shower or bath, do dishes, and handle ordinary laundry without the well running dry. If you already know two loads of laundry empty the well, disclose that. Do not knowingly promise adequate supply.

Sampling rights often sit in the same clause: access for water samples with an acceptable local health-authority rating, and a seller-paid path to correct an unacceptable result before closing when that is what the clause says. Survival language is not automatic unless it is written there.

Potable water means water that is safe to drink under the applicable test and authority standards. Keep quantity, yield, and recovery separate from a bacterial sample. Public Health Ontario's free private-well testing screens for bacterial indicators such as total coliforms and E. coli. It is not a full chemical report. Use your local public health unit's guidance and the lab report in your hand.

Many mortgage files ask for acceptable well-water results before funding. That is lender- and product-specific. Confirm early which tests, how fresh, and what "acceptable" means for that approval. A missing or failed test can jeopardize financing if the lender requires clean results. Test early, and bring in a well professional if quantity, recovery, treatment, or contamination is in doubt.

Chattels, fixtures, and what the warranty covers

A chattel is generally movable, like a freestanding sofa. A fixture is generally attached, like a built-in cabinet. Borderline items are why the inclusions and exclusions lines matter more than the listing photos.

If a seller wants to take an attached fixture, name it clearly in the fixtures excluded list before anyone signs. Example: a dining-room chandelier that was a wedding gift and has sentimental value. It may be a light fixture, but sentimental value does not keep it by itself. A clear exclusion overrides a general "lighting fixtures included" line. There is no implied duty to leave a replacement fixture unless the parties agree in writing. If removal, patching, or making the ceiling safe matters, put that in writing too.

When sellers ask what a chattels and fixtures warranty commits them to, this is the kitchen-table version. Covered items often include the fridge, stove, dishwasher, range hood, washer and dryer, light fixtures, garage-door opener and remotes, central vacuum, and anything else listed as included. The seller typically represents and warrants that chattels, fixtures, and mechanical, electrical, heating, plumbing, ventilation and other equipment will be in good working order on completion.

If a covered fridge or garage-door opener fails before closing, the seller is on the hook to deal with it by completion under that promise. Hypothetical example only: the dishwasher dies ten days before closing. Expect to bear the cost of a proper repair or a suitable replacement, or another written solution both sides agree to. It is not a licence to swap in a cheap substitute the buyer never agreed to.

A representation is a statement of fact. A warranty is a contractual promise. Many clauses use both. Either can create consequences if it is wrong.

Understand that risk before you sign a broad working-order promise. If you are not willing to stand behind an item, or you already know it is faulty, disclose the exact defect and put an item-specific as-is or not-warranted exception in the agreement so it does not fight the broad warranty.

When the clause says the warranty shall survive and not merge on completion, but only for circumstances existing at completion, the promise is about closing day. It is not usually an open-ended guarantee that nothing fails next month. That is why the pre-closing walk-through matters.

Stigmas versus defects

A stigma is usually a non-physical attribute that may affect how a particular buyer feels about a property. RECO examples include a death at the property, a remediated grow-op history, or a notorious former owner. That is different from a latent defect that makes a property unfit, dangerous, or potentially dangerous, which sellers generally must disclose when known.

Sellers are not always required to volunteer every unsolicited stigma detail. If a buyer asks, the seller must not lie or mislead. That does not mean every question automatically forces an answer. A seller may decline some questions, and agents work under client instructions and privacy limits.

I usually recommend putting a stigma clause in the offer and asking clear questions early. The point is protection if something later affects your comfort living there or how easy the home is to resell. That is about marketability and peace of mind, not a guaranteed price-loss formula.

Wording I commonly use has the seller represent and warrant that, to the best of their knowledge, there has never been a murder, death or suicide in the home, or stigmatization in any way that could affect the buyer's decision. Read that as a known-facts statement with a knowledge qualifier, not a guarantee that nothing ever happened beyond the seller's knowledge. Better drafting names the buyer's specific concerns.

Condo status certificate

If you are buying a condo, I recommend a solicitor-review condition on the status certificate package. You own your unit and an interest in the common elements. You do not personally own a pocket of the reserve fund you can withdraw from. Your lawyer should review the corporation's financials, reserve fund and related study or reports where included, known special assessments, rules, what maintenance fees cover, and parking or locker rights. Parking and lockers may be owned, exclusive-use, or leased. Title and the documents decide which.

The Condominium Authority of Ontario notes that a status certificate can include governing documents, budget and financials, reserve-fund information, arrears, special assessments, insurance, and litigation information. Under the Condominium Act, a corporation generally has 10 days to provide a status certificate after a proper request and fee. That statutory clock is different from the negotiated deadlines in your offer.

In the agreements I typically write, the seller requests the package at the seller's expense and we build in production timing that often lines up with about 10 days, then the buyer's lawyer has about three to five banking days after receiving the complete package to review and meet the client. Those buyer-side days are negotiated. Rush service is sometimes available for an extra fee, but it is not promised. Seller-paid ordering is common in my practice. It is not a universal legal rule.

A lawyer can review the paper health of the building. A status package is not a full physical inspection of the complex, and it is not a guarantee that no future special assessment will ever be levied. If the lawyer and client are satisfied, the buyer gives written notice that the condition is fulfilled or waived by the deadline.

HST: included in the price, or in addition?

On the standard Ontario offer form, if the sale is subject to HST, the parties choose whether that tax is included in the purchase price or in addition to it. That only allocates who bears the tax if tax applies. It is not a CRA ruling.

Previously occupied residential housing is usually exempt, with exceptions such as some builder or substantial-renovation situations. Vacant land sold by an individual or personal trust is often exempt when it was personal-use land, but can be taxable if it was used primarily in a business, sold in the course of a business, or sold after the seller subdivided or severed the parcel into more than two parts, subject to related-person and two-part exceptions. A sale to family is not universally exempt. Property held by a corporation does not get the same individual personal-use land exemption. HST on the property is separate from HST on professional services.

For Georgian Bay lots and severances, confirm tax status before you write or accept an offer, then match the included or in addition wording to that advice. Speak with an accountant about your facts, and have your lawyer confirm the contract wording.

The deposit

The deposit is trust money under the agreement, usually held by the listing brokerage and credited on completion. It is different from the total down payment, although it normally counts toward the buyer's funds on closing.

On the standard form, "Upon Acceptance" means the buyer must deliver the deposit to the deposit holder within 24 hours of acceptance, unless the agreement says otherwise. Conditional offers can still require a deposit before conditions are fulfilled. Some deals negotiate a longer period, such as two banking days, in Schedule A.

Follow the brokerage trust instructions for method and payee. Certified cheque, bank draft, or wire are common when accepted. Do not assume a personal cheque is always refused. For wires, verify payment details independently. Starting a transfer is not the same as funds received. If you need more time, negotiate it before acceptance. There is no unilateral grace period. A late deposit can put the buyer in breach. Get legal advice promptly.

Buyer visits

Most offers I write give the buyer two additional visits before closing, not counting the home inspection and appraisal. In my practice they are usually one hour each, at a mutually agreeable time, with the buyer's agent attending. That is negotiated Schedule A language, not a statutory right. The signed clause governs number, timing, purpose, supervision, and access.

Buyers often use the first visit for family or friends, measurements, furniture layout, paint chips, or trades quotes if guests and access are allowed. Tell the seller ahead of time who is coming and why. It is ordinary courtesy. Do not treat the visit as a second home inspection, and do not start unapproved work, paint tests, or anything that damages the property.

Save the final visit for one or two days before closing. Compare the property to the agreed condition, check that included items are present and working as promised, and flag missing items or damage promptly with your agent and lawyer. That is not a chance to reopen the inspection condition, cancel for any reason, or withhold funds outside the agreement. Expect boxes. Ordinary packing mess is not proof the sellers will fail to vacate.

Survey if available

A survey clause is about a drawing of the property boundaries and improvements, not the same thing as a title search or a new reference plan prepared for a severance.

Wording matters. "If in the seller's possession" usually means hand over an existing copy the seller already has, often within a few days of acceptance. Broader language such as "if available" or "reasonable efforts" can push further, depending on the contract, toward searching for or obtaining a copy that can reasonably be obtained. That is not a universal rule that every seller must buy a brand-new survey.

Sellers should understand what they are promising before they sign. You can negotiate a narrow promise: provide an existing survey already in your possession, with no obligation to commission or purchase a new one unless both sides agree. An old survey may not match today's fences, additions, or title picture, and not every useful survey sits in a public land registry ready to download.

Water seepage, leaks, and flooding

I usually try to include a clause that the seller, to the best of their knowledge and belief, knows of no water seepage, leaks, flooding, or water penetration issues, including at the roof or foundation when we negotiate that scope. The point is to put the question on paper instead of assuming a dry showing day means a clean history.

Example: a basement takes on water every spring, but it looks dry during summer showings. You close and take possession in spring, then find water. An express statement about current, recurring, and previous water problems, plus repair records when they exist, gives everyone a clearer record of what was asked and what was promised.

The sample Schedule A uses the office WATER DAMAGE WARRANTY wording: on completion, no known damage to the basement, roof or elsewhere caused by water seepage or flooding, with the usual survive-and-not-merge limit to the state of the property at completion. That is broader than a one-line "no known seepage or flooding" knowledge statement, and still is not automatically a promise about every past repair or a full history beyond what the clause says. Draft the clause to match the concerns you actually care about.

Ask specifically about active leaks, recurring seasonal water, and previous water damage or repairs. Known dangerous or unfit latent defects still sit under Ontario disclosure rules separately from stigma-style issues. Whether an old remediated problem must be disclosed, and what happens if a statement is knowingly false, depends on the facts, the records, the contract wording, and legal advice. A neighbour's story alone does not prove what the seller knew or that a court claim will succeed.

Bottom line

Read Schedule A the way you would read a map before a winter drive up the shore. Conditions buy time. Warranties make dated promises. Disclosures share facts. Deadlines matter. Make the wording fit the property, and lean on your REALTOR®, lawyer, accountant, lender, and inspectors where you need them.
