Below is what I would tell a friend to look for, and I say it knowing it holds me to the same standard. Four things separate agents. None of them is charm, and none of them is how long someone has had a licence.

What actually separates agents

One

Production volume, not years on a business card

Hire a full-time professional. Not someone learning current market conditions on the fly, on your listing, because they sell one or two properties a year alongside another job.

Years are a weak measure and the industry leans on them because they sound impressive. An agent with thirty-five years selling five to ten homes a year has fewer transactions behind them than an agent selling sixty-five a year for seven. The second one has seen more negotiations, more buyer behaviour, and more ways a deal goes sideways. That pattern recognition is what you are actually hiring, and it is also what buys you position and connections inside the market when it comes time to negotiate.

What matters is a proven record of winning in a challenging market, not only a rising one. Anyone looks capable when everything sells.

Ask for the numbers

How many properties did you sell last year?

What is your list-to-sale ratio?

What is your average days on market?

How many of those were in this geographic area?

Two

Real money spent on marketing

Roughly ninety percent of buyers start their search online. An agent who economizes on marketing is economizing on the only place your buyer is looking.

No corners cut means all of this, included, not offered as an upgrade:

  • Professional photography
  • A 3D walkthrough
  • A 2D floor plan tour
  • A staging consultation, included in the service
  • High-quality video, not a phone walkthrough
  • Geographically targeted social posts built to pull traffic
  • Genuine syndication to third-party sites and apps, with accurate content on each one

That last point is worth pressing on. Plenty of agents will tell you a listing is syndicated everywhere when what they mean is it was cross-promoted out of REALTOR.ca and the details on half those sites are wrong or thin. Ask to see it on the actual third-party sites.

Three

They can read the data and tell you the truth about it

If a listing is not getting action, you need an agent who will say so, explain why, and articulate what repositioning it looks like. That conversation is uncomfortable and a lot of agents avoid it until the listing has gone stale.

Doing it properly requires knowing the numbers. How many showings to an offer is normal at this price point? What are days on market doing in this specific pocket? An agent who cannot point to those milestones does not have the big-picture read on how market data is interpreted, which means they cannot help you make a good decision about your own financial future. They can only tell you what happened, not what it means.

Ask

At my price point, how many showings usually produce an offer?

What will you tell me, and when, if we are not getting that?

Four

How fast they say yes to cutting their commission

Commission is negotiable and it is always a matter between you and the agent. This is not about the number. It is about the speed of the answer.

Agents who are genuinely successful in any market tend to have less flexibility, because they believe in what they deliver. If you ask an agent to discount and the answer is an immediate yes, the service almost always shrinks to fit the new budget, and what gets cut first is the marketing from point two.

There is a simpler version of this. If an agent cannot hold their ground on their own compensation, ask yourself how they will hold ground on the price of the largest asset you own.

Then ask the questions that are specific to Tiny

The four above apply anywhere. These do not. Tiny Township has no town centre, roughly seventy kilometres of Georgian Bay shoreline, and almost nothing in common with an in-town sale twenty minutes away.

  1. How many properties have you sold in Tiny Township in the last twelve months, and how many were on water? General Simcoe County volume is a different answer. Ask for the Tiny number.
  2. On this stretch of shoreline, which properties own down to the water and which have township land in between? Ownership does not work the same way twice along Tiny's shoreline. Some properties carry riparian rights. Some are owned outright to the water's edge. Others have a strip between the house and the beach that the Township owns fully or partially. It is the largest single variable in what a waterfront property is worth, and it is not visible on a listing. An agent who works this shoreline answers by address. One who does not will guess.
  3. This home is on a well and septic. Explain to me how they work, what they cost to maintain, and what replacement looks like. This is the question I would weight most heavily, and it is the one most agents fumble. Roughly seven in ten buyers here are coming from the GTA and have never owned either. They do not know what a septic bed is, what it costs to replace, what will ruin one, or what a water test result means. If your agent cannot explain all of that in plain language, they cannot answer a nervous buyer's questions either, and a nervous buyer walks. They also will not spot the problems early, which is where deals die.
  4. What will you have done on the well and septic before we list? The right answer is specific and it happens before the sign goes up: the septic pumped and inspected, and a water test back in hand reading 0.0. Get those early and you control the conversation. Leave them to a buyer's conditions and you are negotiating against a surprise, days from closing, with no leverage and a moving truck booked.
  5. Which beach community is this in, and how does pricing here differ from the one next to it? Balm Beach does not price like Woodland Beach. Cawaja does not price like Wymbolwood. A township-wide average hides all of it.
  6. How will you reach the Toronto buyer searching from three hours away? A large share of Tiny buyers decide from video and photography before they ever drive up.

Interview two or three agents and ask all of them the same questions. The differences show up inside ten minutes.
