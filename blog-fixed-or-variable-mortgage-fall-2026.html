Fixed or Variable? What This Fall's Mortgage Rate Gap Means for Georgian Bay Buyers

By Jonathan Wallace, REALTOR®, Faris Team Real Estate Brokerage · Updated October 2026 · ~7 min read

Thinking of selling? Book a home evaluation

The short answer: As of mid-October 2026, the best five-year fixed mortgage rates sit about 1.1 percentage points above variable rates, the widest gap since 2022. On a $500,000 mortgage, that gap is worth roughly $300 a month. If you can live with a payment that may move, variable deserves a serious look this fall. If you need a payment that never changes, fixed still buys that certainty, but it costs more than it usually does.

If you have been house-hunting in Midland, Penetanguishene or Tiny this fall, you have probably noticed something odd. Your pre-approval rate keeps creeping up, while every headline says the economy is slowing down. Those two things sound like they should not go together, but they are both true right now, and once you see why, you can actually use it to save money on your next purchase or renewal.

Why Fixed Rates Rose While the Economy Cooled

Here is the part most people get backwards. Fixed mortgage rates do not move when the Bank of Canada moves. They follow something called the bond yield. A bond is really just the government borrowing money from investors, and the yield is the interest it pays them for it. Lenders watch that yield closely, because it is roughly what it costs them to fund a fixed mortgage. When the yield goes up, fixed rates go up. Those yields climbed over the summer, so since early September all six of the big banks have nudged their fixed rates up, and some five-year specials are now over 5%. If your fixed quote looks worse than it did in the spring, that is why, and it has nothing to do with the Bank of Canada yet.

Now the other side of the story. The job market is clearly cooling. Canada lost about 68,000 jobs in September, the second month in a row of losses, which puts the two-month total near 110,000. Ontario accounted for about 20,000 of that, while Alberta actually added jobs. Paycheques are growing more slowly too.

This is where variable rates come in, because variable is the one tied to the Bank of Canada. The Bank sets a single key interest rate, and its whole job is to keep inflation in check. When the economy runs hot and prices climb too fast, it raises that rate to cool things down. When the economy weakens and people lose jobs, it tends to lower the rate, or at least hold steady, to avoid squeezing households further. With jobs being lost two months running, the Bank is far less likely to raise at its October 28 decision. Right now the odds of a hike are only about one in three, and the last big clue before then is the inflation report on October 19.

One thing that trips people up: even when the Bank of Canada does change its rate, the banks do not always pass it along right away, or in full. The Bank's rate sets what it costs a bank to borrow, but each bank still prices its own mortgages based on competition, its own funding costs and how much risk it sees in the market. So a cut does not land in your account automatically, and the timing can vary from one lender to the next. It is always worth asking your lender directly how, and when, a change would reach you.

What the 1.1 Point Gap Actually Costs You

Think of it this way. That 1.1 point gap is roughly the same as the Bank of Canada raising rates four and a half times. Go fixed today and you are paying as if all of those increases have already happened. Go variable and you only pay more if the Bank actually raises, and only when it does.

Put real numbers on it. On a $500,000 mortgage paid over 25 years, the payment at 5.14% is about $2,948 a month. At a rate 1.1 points lower, it drops to about $2,641. That is roughly $307 a month back in your pocket, or close to $3,700 a year. These are illustrations, not a quote you can take to the bank, but the size of the gap is real.

None of this means variable is right for everyone. It means the price of certainty is higher than usual right now, and that is worth knowing before you sign.

How to Decide Between Fixed and Variable, Step by Step

  1. Find your monthly ceiling. Write down the highest mortgage payment your household can carry without stress, after property taxes, heating and, for waterfront and rural homes, items like septic, well and insurance.
  2. Run the variable payment at today's rate, then add half a point and a full point. If you can still cover the payment at plus one point, variable is a realistic option for you.
  3. Ask which kind of variable mortgage you are being offered. Some keep your payment the same and adjust how much goes to principal. Others change your payment every time the Bank moves. Know which one you are signing.
  4. Check whether you can lock in later. Many variable mortgages let you convert to a fixed term without a penalty if rates start rising faster than you are comfortable with.
  5. Compare prepayment penalties if there is any chance you will sell early. Breaking a variable mortgage usually costs about three months of interest. Breaking a fixed mortgage can cost far more, which matters for buyers who see a cottage or starter home as a five-year chapter.
  6. Talk it through with a mortgage professional before you commit. Bring your numbers from steps one and two.

What the Rate Picture Means for Our Local Market

Many buyers in North Simcoe are moving up from the GTA, so Toronto's market shapes ours. Toronto home prices slipped to a new low for this cycle in September, and sales fell about 9% from a year earlier. What the headlines missed is supply: new listings dropped about 14% and active inventory fell about 9% over the same period. Fewer homes are coming to market, which helps keep prices from sliding further.

For GTA buyers heading north, softer Toronto prices can mean less equity to bring than expected. Get your current home's value checked early so your purchase budget starts from a real number.

There are also early reports from GTA mortgage and real estate professionals that activity at the upper end of the market is picking up, driven by buyers with significant cash who are less affected by rates. It has not shown up in official data yet, so treat it as something to watch rather than a trend. For Georgian Bay waterfront, these are exactly the buyers who tend to move first when confidence returns.

Finally, household financial stress is real. National consumer insolvency filings in August were the highest for that month since 2009, and Ontario is running ahead of the national pace. That may bring a few more motivated sellers to market over the winter. It is not a crisis, and it does not need to become one, but it is part of the picture for buyers watching for opportunity.

The Bottom Line for Buyers and Homeowners Renewing This Fall

Fixed rates have climbed while the case for further Bank of Canada increases has weakened. That combination has made variable rates more competitive than they have been in years. The right choice still depends on your budget, your comfort with change, and how long you plan to keep the mortgage.

Questions People Ask

Is a fixed or variable mortgage better in fall 2026?

As of mid-October 2026, variable rates are about 1.1 points below the best five-year fixed rates, the widest gap since 2022. Variable is worth considering if your budget can absorb a higher payment. Fixed suits buyers who need a guaranteed payment.

Will the Bank of Canada raise rates on October 28, 2026?

Markets put the odds at about one in three after two straight months of job losses. The October 19 inflation report is the key signal before the decision.

Why are fixed mortgage rates going up?

Fixed rates follow five-year government bond yields, which rose through late summer. All six of Canada's largest banks have raised at least some fixed rates since early September.

How much does the rate gap cost on a $500,000 mortgage?

On a 25-year amortization, a 1.1 point difference works out to roughly $300 a month, or about $3,700 a year.

What is a bond yield and why does it affect my mortgage?

A government bond is a loan from investors to the government, and the yield is the annual return the government pays on it. The five-year Government of Canada bond yield is the benchmark lenders use to price five-year fixed mortgages, because it reflects their own cost of funding that term. When the yield rises, fixed mortgage rates tend to rise within days to weeks; when it falls, fixed rates ease. Variable rates, by contrast, are tied to the Bank of Canada's policy rate rather than to bond yields.

How does the Bank of Canada decide whether to change interest rates?

The Bank sets its policy interest rate to keep inflation near its 2% target. When inflation runs hot or the economy overheats, it raises the rate to slow borrowing and spending; when the economy weakens or unemployment rises, it lowers or holds the rate to support activity. It meets on a fixed schedule, eight times a year, and weighs data such as the Labour Force Survey and the monthly Consumer Price Index. The next decision is October 28, 2026, with the key inflation reading landing October 19.

If the Bank of Canada cuts its rate, will my bank lower my rate right away?

Not always, and not always by the full amount. The policy rate sets what it costs banks to borrow, but each lender prices its own mortgages based on competition, its funding costs and its read on risk. Variable-rate holders usually see a change within a billing cycle or two, while fixed rates move with bond yields instead. Ask your lender exactly how and when any change would reach you.
