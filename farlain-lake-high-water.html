Farlain Lake’s high water: what the science says, what residents are living with, and where solutions stand

By Jonathan Wallace, Realtor, Faris Team Real Estate Brokerage · September 26, 2026 · ~9 min read

Like this article? Join the weekly newsletter

Farlain Lake sits in Tiny Township, inland from Georgian Bay near Awenda Provincial Park. For years, shoreline owners have watched the water climb. Beaches shrink. Trees die. Septics sit in wet ground. In spring 2026, the Farlain Lake Community Association said levels reached the highest in memory, and Severn Sound Environmental Association (SSEA) gauge readings backed that up with numbers, not just photos (FLCA 2026 updates; FLCA: Highest Lake Level in Memory).

This is not a viral mystery. It is a documented kettle-lake problem with real people on both sides of the shoreline, and a slow municipal process that frustrates almost everyone. Here is what the public record actually says.

Why this lake behaves differently

Farlain Lake is a seepage kettle lake. It has no surface outlet. Water comes in from groundwater, a year-round groundwater-fed tributary, intermittent streams after rain, and runoff from a watershed much larger than the lake (about 14 km² of catchment for roughly 1 km² of lake). Water leaves mainly by evaporation and slow groundwater flow. SSEA’s January 2019 memo to Tiny Township says the lake likely discharges very slowly toward Second Lake (also called Kettle’s Lake) in Awenda Park through groundwater, not through a ditch you can see (SSEA Farlain Lake Water Levels, Jan 2019; also Appendix A in Tiny PWR-022-25).

That design matters. In wet years, water stacks up. In dry years, groundwater still feeds the lake, so it does not drop the way a lake with a river outlet might. Surface sands can move water into the lake quickly. A silt-and-clay layer underneath (Sunnybrook drift) slows the way out. Tiny’s Public Works report in August 2025 repeated that framing and noted that even in a dry summer, levels stayed high, possibly after extreme winter snowfall (PWR-022-25, CivicWeb).

The numbers, not just the stories

Volunteer spring readings summarized by SSEA showed a substantial rise after 2015, including roughly a 40 cm jump from spring 2015 to spring 2016 (SSEA 2019). A public gauge went in near the Andrew Drive boat launch in 2019 through SSEA, Tiny, and FLCA volunteers (FLCA gauge news).

SSEA’s later updates put 2026 in context relative to that gauge (relative depth, not a Georgian Bay flood datum):

  • Early May 2026 readings around 0.76 m, about 32 cm above the historic May average for the gauge series
  • Still elevated into late summer: mid-August and mid-September 2026 readings around 0.63 m, well above historic averages for those months
  • 2025 already had the highest annual average in the 2019 to 2025 gauge series before the 2026 spring peak

Those figures come from SSEA’s public Water Level Watch materials and 2026 combined updates (SSEA monitoring results; Water Level Watch). The FLCA’s longer narrative matches the arc: rise since about 2016, a high around 2019, spring 2023 still near that high, and 2026 described as the highest in memory (FLCA Lake Water Levels).

What residents are living with

Public documents and community updates describe the same cluster of impacts, especially on lower-lying east and south shores and around the Andrew Drive launch:

  • Lost beach and usable yard
  • Mature shoreline trees dying (and expensive, unsafe removals)
  • Flooded or threatened septics, wells, docks, and boathouses
  • Harder or unsafe launch use (including submerged hazards)
  • High water tables and sump stress on some back-lot homes as well as shoreline lots

Tiny staff say many homes were built close to the water before modern zoning, and that flooded septics can pollute the lake. They also note municipal impacts: less park space and boat-launch usability on Andrew Drive, and about 200 metres of Andrew Drive with a shorter pavement life. They say, from staff knowledge, roads have not had to close because of Farlain high water (PWR-022-25). SSEA’s 2019 memo estimated up to about 190 homes or cottages could be affected depending on how close structures sit to the shore.

In 2026 the FLCA High-Water Group asked owners (shoreline and back lot) to document damage, photos, and costs for advocacy with the township and agencies (FLCA 2026 page). Local news put faces on the file. Simcoe.com / Midland Mirror coverage quoted shoreline owners describing the highest water they had ever seen, lost beach, and pressure for a culvert or a faster municipal fix (Simcoe.com, Apr 2026; earlier Tiny Farlain coverage). MidlandToday covered a June 2026 council deputation from Madelaine Gallagher of Andrew Drive: a deep waterfront lot reduced to a thin strip of usable depth, boathouse gone, water in the basement, and a call to declare an emergency, fund a hydrogeological study, and provide immediate sandbags and pumping. Dozens of Farlain supporters attended (MidlandToday).

That frustration is real. So is the township’s answer that there is no quick fix.

Ideas that sound simple (and why staff and scientists push back)

Some residents argue that Farlain Lake Road at the north end (municipal road in the 1980s; a private trespass road existed earlier) blocked a former path toward Second Lake, and that removing it or adding culverts would help. Tiny’s 2025 report pushes back with air photos and elevations: a 1931 photo predates the township road and does not show the lake extending farther north, and the land between Farlain and Second rises more than about 6 metres (20 feet) above Farlain’s water. Staff say draining would mean pumping or a pipe 600 m or longer, if it is even legal and feasible (PWR-022-25).

SSEA’s 2019 advice is clear: it does not recommend creating an artificial channel. Cutting north into Awenda or east toward Penetang Harbour could harm water quality and temperature, change the fluctuation native plants need, and move invasive species. Approvals under fisheries and related rules would be hard. The FLCA has also summarized park direction that drainage through Awenda into Second Lake is not on the table (SSEA 2019; FLCA Lake Water Levels).

Official materials do not treat beaver dams as the controlling cause. The consistent staff and SSEA framing is: no surface outlet, precipitation and snowmelt, and local geology.

None of that means “do nothing forever.” It means a simple ditch is not the engineering path on the public record.

Possible resolutions on the public record

Tiny’s August 2025 staff report laid out four options (PWR-022-25):

  1. Do nothing. Cheap for the general taxpayer. Does nothing for shoreline owners. Staff said the issue would keep returning.
  2. Fund a hydrogeological study (roughly $120,000 in 2026 budget talk) with SSEA or consultants. Better science. Does not by itself lower the lake.
  3. Educate residents on a Drainage Act petition (staff’s recommended option). A public meeting with the drainage superintendent. If a valid petition arrives, an engineer studies options, costs, and who pays.
  4. Township petitions as road authority. Similar study path, with different cost and control trade-offs. Preliminary engineer’s report estimated around $80,000.

Council directed staff toward Option 3. The township then held a Public Information Centre on 25 November 2025 (in person and online). Recording: YouTube. Official pages: PIC event and PIC news update. Feedback email: farlainwaterlevels@tiny.ca.

An FLCA summary prepared after the PIC (in consultation with the township) is worth reading if you own on the lake (PIC summary PDF):

  • Further Drainage Act work depends on a community petition
  • Public materials describe a high bar for shoreline support (FLCA summary: about 60% of owners of roughly 225 shoreline properties, township-owned lots aside; the staff report also cites Drainage Act language about properties “requiring drainage”)
  • Council then decides whether to accept the petition and appoint an engineer
  • Study and any works get paid through a benefit assessment across the watershed, including the township as a property owner. Shoreline and back-lot owners can both be in the net

MidlandToday later quoted Public Works director Tim Leitch saying that after residents were walked through the petition path, he had received zero inquiries from lake residents to engage a petition, while Mayor David Evans said there is “no panacea” and urged people to put their names on the process (MidlandToday).

Both things can be true at once. Flooding is urgent for the people in it. Ontario drainage law is not a sandbag program.

Where progress stands (late September 2026)

What has happened

  • Years of FLCA / SSEA monitoring and public reporting
  • SSEA 2019 technical memo and later gauge updates (including 2023 and 2026)
  • June 2025 community pressure, then the August 2025 staff report, then Council Option 3
  • November 2025 Public Information Centre and dedicated township email
  • 2026 record relative highs, community damage documentation, and continued council advocacy
  • Practical mitigation: township sand and sandbags made available through FLCA (including a late-September 2026 free sand-and-bags notice) (FLCA sand and sandbags)

What has not clearly happened in the public materials reviewed for this piece

  • No artificial outlet built
  • No completed hydrogeological “water budget” study at the roughly $120,000 Option 2 scope
  • No confirmed Drainage Act petition filed and accepted (as of the June 2026 “zero inquiries” statement, no contrary public filing was found through late September 2026 searches)
  • No Farlain-specific emergency declaration; FLCA reported provincial assistance declined in spring 2026 messaging

If you live on Farlain Lake, the practical next steps in the public record are still: document impacts with the FLCA, write farlainwaterlevels@tiny.ca, use sandbag supports when offered, watch for township follow-up reports, and decide as a community whether to pursue a formal Drainage Act petition knowing costs may land on benefitting owners.

A note for buyers and sellers (not legal advice)

If you are buying or selling on Farlain Lake, treat high water as a disclosure and diligence issue, not a rumour. Ask about septic location and flood history, shoreline structures, insurance, sump history on back lots, and whether the seller has joined any petition or FLCA reporting effort. Read the township, SSEA, and FLCA materials yourself. A sale closed in a quieter year may not describe a 2026 shoreline.
